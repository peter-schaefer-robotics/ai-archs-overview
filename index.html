How to read this

Year is the first publication or release, not the year the idea became popular. Backpropagation is a good example: the maths dates to 1970, the paper that made everyone use it is 1986.

A few entries are training methods or kernels rather than architectures (RLHF, GRPO, FlashAttention, PPO). They are in here because they changed what architectures were possible, which is the question this table is really about.

The ◆ milestone mark is for the 23 entries that reset the default for everything after them: a new thing became possible, and within a few years most of the field had rebuilt around it. Useful ideas that stayed in their niche (KAN, capsules, normalizing flows) and refinements of an existing idea (Swin, GRU, MLA) are not marked, however good they are. The mark is a judgement call, not a measurement.

The Source column links the paper or release that introduced each entry, so every row can be checked at the primary source rather than taken on trust.

Entries from 2026 are weeks to months old. Treat the frontier rows as a snapshot, not settled history: linear-attention parity at trillion scale in particular is still contested, and MiniMax reverting from linear attention in M1 back to full attention in M2 is the cautionary data point.

Three patterns worth noticing

  • Bottlenecks move, they don't disappear. RNNs were gradient-bound, Transformers are memory-bound, sparse models are HBM-bound. Each fix exposes the next wall.
  • Removing a hand-built prior usually wins, eventually. Hand-crafted features → CNNs, alignment tables → attention, convolutions → ViT, tokenizers → byte-level models. Each needed more data than anyone expected.
  • 2024–2026 is about spending compute selectively rather than uniformly: sparse experts, sparse attention, conditional memory, and thinking longer only when the question is hard.
